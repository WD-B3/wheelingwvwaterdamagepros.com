A residential island in the Ohio River with more than a thousand historic buildings.

A residential island in the Ohio River

Wheeling Island lies in the Ohio River just west of downtown Wheeling, and the City of Wheeling lists it in its 2nd Ward. The Wheeling Island Historic District, listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1992, covers roughly 370 acres and 1,110 contributing buildings. The listing describes it as largely residential, made up mainly of two story, frame, detached houses built from the mid 19th to the early 20th century.

The styles named include Italianate, Queen Anne, Colonial Revival and Bungalow or Craftsman. Water damage restoration on the island often means drying a wood frame house, where water can travel inside wall cavities and under floors before any stain shows.

Why frame houses need readings, not guesses

In a frame house, a leak from a second floor bath can run down inside a wall and soak insulation, framing and the ceiling below. The surface may look fine for days. A moisture meter and thermal camera show where the water went, and drying equipment is placed from that map. The structural drying page explains the equipment and the readings.

The same goes for basements and crawl spaces, if your house has one. Do not assume. Look at what is below you, and tell the dispatcher what you find.

The river and flood history

The Ohio River record crest at Wheeling was 55.20 feet on March 19, 1936, more than 15 feet above flood stage, according to the West Virginia Encyclopedia. The same source lists other major floods in 1884, 1898, 1907, 1913, 1937 and 1942. Those are citywide river events.

We did not find a source that breaks out damage for individual island streets, so this page does not claim any. If river levels are rising, follow the National Weather Service and Wheeling emergency management, not a restoration website. Neighborhood-specific FEMA flood-zone status not verified. Check the FEMA Flood Map Service Center for your address.

Everyday water damage on the island

  • A supply line or fitting that splits and soaks a floor..
  • A water heater or washer that leaks while no one is home..
  • A drain or sewer line that backs up and brings contaminated water into the house..
  • A roof or window leak during heavy rain..

Clean water and contaminated water are handled differently. If the water came from a drain, toilet or river, keep people and pets out and call before touching anything. The sewage backup page explains why household fans and ordinary vacuums are not enough.

Safe first steps

  1. Leave and call 911 for gas smells, rising water or any immediate danger..
  2. Do not stand in water near outlets, cords or appliances..
  3. Shut off the water supply only when it is safe and reachable..
  4. Photograph the damage and the source from a dry spot..
  5. Call for help before moving wet materials out of the house..

Records for your insurer

Take photos before anything moves and keep a dated list of damaged belongings. Moisture readings from the crew show how far the water went and how drying progressed. Whether the loss is covered depends on the cause and the policy, and flood from a river is often handled under a different policy than a sudden pipe break. We do not decide coverage.
